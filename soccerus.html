1

Kids born in the same year can be up to six years apart in physical development

US youth soccer groups players by birth year, so a January birthday and a December birthday share a team. On top of that, kids mature at different rates: in one large academy study, some boys in the same age group were up to six years apart biologically, and one 15-year-old had the body of an 18-year-old. If your child looks small next to teammates, birth year may be the wrong comparison.

Same age group, very different bodies
2

Academies tend to pick the early developers, and the gap grows every year

A 2025 study of 1,011 players at 12 professional academies found that selection favored early-maturing boys from U12 upward. The bias was small at U12 and large by U18. Many coaches still reward size without realizing it.

Bias towards early developers, by age group
3

Late developers are the players most likely to slip through

Researchers found late developers were heavily underrepresented in academies, even though many of them catch up physically later on. In the academy system that study covered, no late-maturing players were left by age 14. Knowing which group your child is in helps you read a tryout result for what it really is.

4

U.S. Soccer backs bio-banding, but it isn't required

U.S. Soccer introduced a bio-banding initiative in 2018, grouping players by maturity instead of age. It has said bio-banding will never be mandatory, so whether your child is ever assessed depends entirely on their club. For most club, travel and rec players, the answer is never.

You can check it at home in five minutes, with no X-ray

The report uses the Khamis-Roche method, published in the journal Pediatrics and developed on American children. All you need is a tape measure, a bathroom scale and both parents' heights.

It tells you when the growth spurt is coming

Around their fastest growth, many young players go through a clumsy spell and growth-related aches around the knees and heels. Seeing it coming means you can mention it to the coach early and help your player stay patient while their body catches up.

Questions parents ask

How accurate is it?

In the original study, 9 in 10 predictions landed within about 2.1 inches of actual adult height for boys and 1.7 inches for girls. It was developed on healthy children in the United States, so treat it as a well-founded estimate. Careful measurements make the biggest difference, and the order page shows you how.

Will it tell me if my child will make it?

No. Height and maturity are one part of the picture, and plenty of top players were late developers. The report helps you understand why your child might look ahead of or behind teammates right now, and what is likely to change.

Is this medical advice?

No. It's an educational estimate. If your child has ongoing pain, very fast or very slow growth, or a condition affecting growth, speak to your pediatrician.
